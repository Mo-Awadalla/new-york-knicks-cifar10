# A concrete, bounded route from 88.4% toward 90%

## Bottom line

There is no evidence-backed recipe that guarantees 90% on this competition's hidden evaluation set. The current 88.40% public score is from a 4,298,970-parameter, full-data student; 97.44% (4,872/5,000) is from the related 45K-training-row recipe/checkpoint, not that same full-data checkpoint. The official [competition Overview](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/cifar-10-res-net-architecture-challenge-dl/overview) describes a separate, held-out image-classification dataset but does not identify its source. The public portion is only 1,000 examples. The 9.04-point numerical difference is not a measured generalization gap for one fixed model, and it identifies neither a particular distribution shift nor an inference bug.

The lowest-risk bounded, no-training action is one frozen identity-plus-horizontal-flip comparison on the existing 45K student and its fixed 5K validation split: average `softmax(f(x))` and `softmax(f(horizontal_flip(x)))` equally, using the same weights and unchanged normalization state for both views. It is the simplest label-preserving CIFAR symmetry and costs two forward views, though the cited papers do not quantify this exact policy on our student. The 5K split has been reused in earlier recipe/model selection, but has not been used for this TTA comparison; report paired per-ID outcomes and uncertainty. The 32-view AugMix result below is higher-upside evidence, but its much weaker model and different policy do not justify making it the primary route.

Do not turn a negative paired flip comparison into a broad crop/flip sweep. The deterministic 10-crop grid described below is a reference alternative, not the policy behind the strongest frozen-TTA result. The 32-view AugMix paper result is worth noting but is not a direct forecast for this stronger CutMix/distilled student. SWA requires trajectory snapshots and a different training schedule; it is not free post-processing on one final checkpoint. Neither calibration nor BN refresh alone is an accuracy intervention.

## What the primary evidence actually supports

### Frozen crop/flip test-time averaging

Molchanov et al., *Greedy Policy Search: A Simple Baseline for Learnable Test-Time Augmentation* (UAI 2020), report CIFAR-10 results for test-time averaging of random crops and horizontal flips (CF) in their [paper and supplementary results](https://arxiv.org/html/2002.09103). Their CIFAR models were trained for 2,000 epochs with a modified RandAugment policy (three operations, magnitudes sampled uniformly from 0 to 45) and standard crop/flip augmentation; they used a stratified 45K/5K split to search policies and then retrained on the full training data. The reported accuracy changes from central-crop inference to CF averaging are:

| CIFAR-10 model | Published parameters | Central crop | CF, 5 views | CF, 10 views | CF, 100 views |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| PreResNet-110 | 1.70M | 97.17% | 97.50% (+0.33 pp) | 97.54% (+0.37 pp) | 97.61% (+0.44 pp) |
| VGG | Not specified in that table | 96.86% | 97.09% (+0.23 pp) | 97.19% (+0.33 pp) | 97.23% (+0.37 pp) |
| WideResNet-28x10 | 36.48M | 98.04% | 98.36% (+0.32 pp) | 98.39% (+0.35 pp) | 98.45% (+0.41 pp) |

The PreResNet-110 is under the course's 5M cap; the WideResNet-28x10 is not. The model counts are from the [CIFAR model implementation's published table](https://github.com/bearpaw/pytorch-classification). These are CIFAR-10 test results from distinct, long-trained models—not the course's distilled WRN-22-4, and not this competition's unknown public set. The paper's CF views use random crop/flip sampling; its effect sizes are therefore not measurements of the exact deterministic two-view wrapper recommended above. The larger number of views gives diminishing returns in these examples; even the 100-view gains are below half a point.

For CIFAR images the authors distinguish the input from ImageNet-style multi-crop: their supplement says CIFAR's 32×32 images are used unmodified at test time and describes horizontal flip as the original-plus-flipped prediction ensemble. Their reported CF experiment also uses crop/flip sampling. Thus horizontal flip is the least invasive frozen policy; crop-based views should be treated as a separate, more speculative candidate. The usual reason to avoid arbitrary crops is practical: a crop can remove class evidence, so averaging need not help every example. The same TTA paper reports that a learned GPS policy improves calibrated log-likelihood but gives accuracy comparable to baselines on CIFAR-10; its CIFAR-10 table does not show a large accuracy jump from an elaborate learned policy over the crop/flip baseline.
### Stronger frozen-TTA result on CIFAR-10.1

Zhang, Levine, and Finn's *MEMO: Test Time Robustness via Adaptation and Augmentation* (NeurIPS 2022) reports a non-adaptive `+TTA` baseline separately from single-point BatchNorm adaptation and MEMO. The definition of this TTA baseline is the marginal prediction, the average of the model's probability distributions over augmented views; it does not update model parameters. In Table 1, their ResNet-26's errors change from 9.2% to 7.3% on CIFAR-10 (−1.9 percentage points), 18.4% to 14.8% on CIFAR-10.1 (−3.6 pp), and 22.5% to 19.9% mean error on CIFAR-10-C (−2.6 pp). The CIFAR-10.1 result is the strongest directly relevant natural-recollection TTA effect found here and numerically exceeds the 1.6 points separating this public score from 90%; it is not evidence that this competition's held-out data are CIFAR-10.1 or that the gain transfers to our student.

The authors' supplement specifies 32 AugMix views for CIFAR ResNet-26 and says the `+TTA` baseline averages the same AugMix views as MEMO. This row is non-adaptive: it performs no optimizer/gradient update and no BatchNorm adaptation. MEMO itself is different: it adapts the model parameters by a test-example-specific entropy gradient step. On CIFAR-10.1, its result reduces error only another 0.1 point versus frozen `+TTA` (14.7% versus 14.8%); that extra change must not be attributed to TTA. The paper's CIFAR ResNet-26 uses GroupNorm, not BatchNorm, so there are no running BatchNorm statistics to update.

The [authors' AugMix helper](https://github.com/zhangmarvin/memo/blob/master/cifar-10-exps/utils/third_party.py) makes each of the 32 views by first applying `RandomCrop(32, padding=4)` (Torchvision's default constant-zero padding) and `RandomHorizontalFlip(p=0.5)`. It then mixes three augmentation chains with weights sampled from `Dirichlet([1,1,1])`; each chain has a random depth of 1–3 operations sampled with replacement from nine operations (autocontrast, equalize, rotate, solarize, shear-x/y, translate-x/y, posterize). Each operation is called with level 1, with internal magnitudes randomized by the helper; rotation/shear/translation use fill value 128. A `Beta(1,1)` coefficient mixes the preaugmented clean image with the three-chain mixture. Thus each AugMix view includes a clean *cropped/flipped* component, but the paper defines TTA as an average over 32 sampled augmentations and does not specify an additional standalone clean prediction as a 33rd view.

Do not treat the author's helper as a drop-in transform for the course model. It normalizes with fixed mean/std 0.5, whereas this student must use its checkpoint's training-row statistics; the project's existing `_augmix_view` also uses a different severity setting. The linked [`test_adapt.py`](https://github.com/zhangmarvin/memo/blob/master/cifar-10-exps/test_calls/test_adapt.py) is MEMO adaptation code: it backpropagates an entropy loss and takes an SGD step for each test example, so it must **not** be invoked for the frozen, non-adaptive TTA baseline. Any such frozen wrapper would need `eval`/no-grad inference, unchanged parameters and normalization state, the checkpoint's train statistics, and the mean of 32 probability vectors. The authors' README says the repository is cleaned from the result-generating version, so use the paper/supplement as authority for the reported score.

This establishes that frozen TTA can help on a separately collected CIFAR-like natural test set without changing weights or BN statistics. It does not establish that AugMix is harmless or beneficial for this CutMix/distilled WRN-22-4, and CIFAR-10.1 is not an identity claim about the competition evaluation set. The effect is one model/recipe result without reported seed-level uncertainty; the 32-view computation and stochastic transform policy are also materially different from a single flip average.

The 3.6-point CIFAR-10.1 error reduction is numerically large enough to motivate attention, but it came from a 366,938-parameter GroupNorm model with 81.6% baseline accuracy on that set and a stochastic 32-view policy. Our 4.3M BatchNorm student is stronger on its internal validation, uses CutMix/distillation, and has not been evaluated with that AugMix policy. Treat the result as evidence that frozen augmentation can help, not evidence that this policy will add 3.6 points to our student. It does not outweigh the two-view flip as the lowest-risk first check.


**Reference only, not the bounded next test: a deterministic 10-view multicrop policy.** Pad each 32×32 image by four pixels, take the center and four corner 32×32 crops, add the horizontal reflection of each, run all 10 views through the same model in evaluation mode, and average the 10 softmax probability vectors. Use the run's existing normalization statistics on each view. This matches the project's crop/flip training transformations but is not the random AugMix policy behind the CIFAR-10.1 result or the random CF protocol in Molchanov et al. This split has been reused in prior model selection and is not a fresh, untouched holdout. Do not test multiple crop grids or tune crop positions, weights, or view count on competition images or leaderboard feedback.

**Recommended frozen policy:** run the same student once on the original 32×32 image and once on its horizontal reflection; average the two softmax probability vectors 50/50. Keep the model in evaluation mode, use the same training-derived normalization on both views, and do not update weights or BatchNorm buffers. The shared model remains 4,298,970 trainable parameters; no learned aggregation weights are added. A positive effect is unknown until paired on validation.

### SWA, EMA, and BatchNorm refresh

The original SWA paper and [authors' CIFAR implementation/results](https://github.com/timgaripov/swa) provide a stronger controlled CIFAR signal than generic claims that averaging "adds a point":

| CIFAR-10 model | Parameters | SGD | SWA, 1 budget | SWA, 1.25 budgets | SWA, 1.5 budgets |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| PreResNet-164 | 1.7M | 95.28 ± 0.10% | 95.56 ± 0.11% (+0.28 pp) | 95.77 ± 0.04% (+0.49 pp) | 95.83 ± 0.03% (+0.55 pp) |
| WideResNet-28x10 | 36.48M | 96.18 ± 0.11% | 96.45 ± 0.11% (+0.27 pp) | 96.64 ± 0.08% (+0.46 pp) | 96.79 ± 0.05% (+0.61 pp) |

The PreResNet-164 result is within the parameter cap; WRN-28x10 is not. The author's CIFAR recipe uses a constant or cyclical late learning rate and averages several trajectory checkpoints. For the 1.5-budget PreResNet result, the SWA run is 225 epochs after a 150-epoch SGD budget, starts averaging at epoch 126, and uses LR 0.01 (initial LR 0.1, weight decay 3e-4). For WRN the 1.5-budget recipe is 300 epochs after a 200-epoch budget. These are small-to-moderate gains under different model/training recipes; the 4.3M student uses CutMix and distillation and has no matched SWA comparison. The project's prior observation that one 400-epoch student had 4,864 versus 4,872 validation-correct is not a SWA comparison and does not settle whether a proper SWA schedule would help.

SWA averages weights from a trajectory with a modified learning-rate schedule; a lone final checkpoint cannot be converted into the authors' SWA result. If a trajectory is run, the averaged weights have to be followed by a BatchNorm-statistics refresh using permitted **training** images. The [PyTorch SWA guidance](https://pytorch.org/blog/stochastic-weight-averaging-in-pytorch/) explains that the averaged weights were not used during training and therefore have no corresponding running activation statistics; it recommends one pass over the training loader after averaging. Recomputing BatchNorm statistics is a correctness step for the averaged model, not an independently demonstrated accuracy gain. Recompute from the training split only, not the 5K validation set or hidden evaluation images.

EMA is also trajectory averaging, not test-time adaptation. Loh et al., [*On the Importance of Calibration in Semi-Supervised Learning*](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.04783), report PAWS+EMA on CIFAR-10: +0.4 points with 250 labels and +0.5 with 2,500 labels (PAWS+SWA: +0.4 and +0.3, respectively). These are semi-supervised PAWS runs with unlabeled-data training and a different setting, not evidence for the project's fully labeled distilled student. The authors use a separate average of model weights; their EMA momentum schedules warm from zero to 0.996 or use a cosine schedule. This is not a reason to add an EMA claim to the current recipe without a matched run.

### Calibration and validation selection

Temperature scaling is useful for confidence/NLL comparisons, but a single positive temperature applied to all logits preserves each example's argmax. It cannot by itself improve top-1 accuracy or get the public score to 90%. Molchanov et al. show calibrated log-likelihood (cLL) to be a better objective than raw accuracy or raw log-likelihood when *searching* many TTA policies, but also report that accuracy is noisy and that their learned policy's CIFAR-10 accuracy is comparable to simpler TTA. A validation-selected temperature or crop policy is still a fit to the validation labels; it does not reveal the hidden test distribution.

The project's old 0.5-point gate is a heuristic, not a course rule or a significance threshold. At 5,000 validation examples, 0.5 points is 25 net additional correct predictions. For a candidate and incumbent on the same IDs, the relevant uncertainty is paired: count candidate-only correct and incumbent-only correct examples, and use a paired interval/test (or a paired bootstrap over IDs). An aggregate accuracy delta alone hides how many predictions changed in each direction. A fixed margin does not account for training-seed variability, selection among many recipes/checkpoints, or the practical value of the compute cost. Predeclare one candidate and one metric before the comparison; do not change the decision rule after inspecting results. The current 97.44% score is 128 errors; a +0.5 point threshold would require 4,897/5,000 (97.94%), but that arithmetic does not establish that 25 net examples is statistically meaningful.

## Why 88.4% versus 97.44% identifies neither cause nor ceiling

These numbers differ in at least three ways: the 97.44% result belongs to a 45K-train/5K-validation recipe checkpoint, whereas the submitted 88.40% checkpoint was retrained on all 50K rows and has no held-out score; the internal and public samples are different; and the public score aggregates only the 1,000 public examples, not all 2,000 evaluation images. The official Overview describes the evaluation as a separate, held-out image-classification dataset but does not identify its source. The public sample may be harder by chance, differ in class mix, or reflect some unmeasured data-generation difference. A scoring or preprocessing mismatch was initially a hypothesis, not something inferred from the score gap. Subsequent local pipeline evidence weighs against a CPU/GPU export or preprocessing discrepancy for the checked checkpoint: a CPU predict-only CSV for all 2,000 rows was byte-identical to the original GPU-trained small-KD prediction CSV (reported SHA-256 prefix/suffix `e25f03b6…92a77d`). Both paths use RGB 32×32 inputs scaled by 1/255 and the checkpoint's training-row normalization statistics; predictions are joined by manifest IDs and emitted in the official sample order. No export/preprocessing bug is evidenced by these checks. They do not test hidden labels or identify the evaluation source, and do not prove every possible pipeline or scoring cause is excluded.

Recht et al.'s [CIFAR-10.1 study](https://arxiv.org/html/1806.00451) shows that a separately collected, approximately 2,000-image CIFAR-10 test set can yield 4–10 point drops for models relative to the original test set, with rankings often mostly preserved. That demonstrates that a sizable gap is possible under one carefully studied natural re-collection; it does **not** identify this competition's 2,000 images as CIFAR-10.1, or prove any particular shift here. Similar sample count and accuracy gap are not dataset identity evidence.

Likewise, there is no demonstrated hard accuracy ceiling for the 4.3M network. A single strong validation score, one public score from a different full-data retrain, and one longer-run validation decline do not bound what the architecture can achieve. There is no theorem here that the parameter cap creates a 90% ceiling, and the hidden distribution is not known. The supportable claim is narrower: this specific full-data candidate scored 88.40% on the public leaderboard; no verified result in this evidence establishes 90%.

For differences between public leaderboard scores, 1,000 public examples mean 0.1 percentage point per example. If per-example labels/predictions were available, compare candidates with a paired method such as McNemar's test; the leaderboard exposes only an aggregate score, not the discordant counts needed for that test. Small public deltas therefore cannot be treated as decisive paired evidence, and multiple candidate selection on that feedback risks adapting to the public subset. The internal 5K paired comparison is the better available selection evidence.

## Rules and parameter-count interpretation

A frozen transform/average is different from test-time adaptation. The proposed TTA only applies deterministic label-preserving input transforms and averages outputs; the trained student, BatchNorm running statistics, and all weights stay fixed, with no gradients, pseudo-labels, test-batch normalization, or optimizer update. Test-time adaptation methods that update weights or normalization statistics using unlabeled hidden evaluation data are a separate category and are not proposed here; they require explicit clearance and would not be a permissible shortcut under the stated no-hidden-label/test-statistics constraints.

**Parameter count and staff confirmation.** Kaggle Rules §4 caps the final model at 5,000,000 trainable parameters, including the classifier and all trainable components. Under the natural shared-weight interpretation, `g(x) = mean_k f_θ(t_k(x))` is still one 4,298,970-parameter student: fixed transforms and averaging have no trainable weights, and the same `θ` is applied on each view. This is not an ensemble of separate 4.3M models, which would have more than 5M total parameters if all models contribute to the final prediction. Replacing the one model's weights by one SWA/EMA average likewise leaves one model of the same size; averaging outputs from multiple independently trained checkpoints is a different, multi-model final predictor. The rules do not name TTA explicitly, so staff should confirm that this frozen inference wrapper is acceptable; the shared-`θ` count is a reasoned interpretation of §4, not a staff ruling. The first recommended flip wrapper avoids learned calibration scalars and view weights.

**Public score feedback.** Kaggle Rules §3 bars using evaluation data for training, validation, or model selection outside the official Kaggle process; §11 says the public leaderboard is intended to provide feedback. Accordingly, ordinary selection among meaningful candidates using the official aggregate public score through Kaggle's submission/leaderboard process is distinct from extracting per-image hidden labels and is within the stated official-process exception. This is not a license to probe: do not submit systematic variants, reconstruct labels/identities, or use test-image statistics. The public score covers 1,000 examples and reveals no paired discordant counts, so it remains weak evidence for small candidate differences.

## Bounded recommendation

1. Obtain staff confirmation that fixed, frozen shared-weight TTA is permitted under the final-model rule (the parameter count remains 4,298,970 under the natural interpretation above).
2. If staff clears fixed TTA, compare the single-view checkpoint once against the two-view identity-plus-horizontal-flip probability average on the fixed 5K validation split. This split has been reused in earlier recipe/model selection and is not a fresh, untouched holdout, but it has not been used for this TTA comparison. Save paired per-ID correctness, candidate-only/incumbent-only counts, accuracy, NLL, and a paired uncertainty interval. Do not change the policy after seeing results.
3. If that comparison supports a gain, apply exactly the same frozen wrapper to the full-data student and make no claim of 90% until an actual official score establishes it. If it does not, keep the present single-view candidate; do not start a broad crop/augmentation or EMA/SWA search in response to one failure.

This plan has the strongest support-to-risk ratio: it adds no training or trainable components and uses the least invasive CIFAR flip symmetry. The best direct crop/flip results on other CIFAR-10 models are around +0.2 to +0.4 points; the larger frozen AugMix result on CIFAR-10.1 is real but too model- and policy-specific to forecast here. Nothing in this evidence guarantees 90%. With the verified Overview deadline of Fri Nov 6, 2026, 23:59:59 GMT-0500, a fresh SWA/EMA search is not the bounded next step.
